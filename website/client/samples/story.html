Addie Brown

Domestic worker whose peripatetic lifestyle reflects the restrictive nature of employment for African American women in 19th-century Hartford.

1841 – 1870 Migration Working Class

A Life in Motion

Addie Brown's story is one of resilience and constant movement. Born in the mid-19th century, her life in Hartford, Connecticut, provides a window into the lived experiences of African American women who navigated a landscape defined by both opportunity and severe structural limitations.

Through her extensive correspondence with Rebecca Primus, Addie documented the challenges of domestic labor. She moved frequently between households, seeking better wages and treatment, illustrating the "peripatetic" nature of survival for Black working-class women during this era. Her mobility wasn't just physical movement across the city, but a strategic negotiation for dignity and independence.

"I am tired of living this way, Addie wrote. I want a home of my own, where I can be free to do as I please."

The Hartford Context

Hartford in the 1860s was a city of stark contrasts. As one of America's wealthiest cities at the end of the Civil War, its prosperity was built on industries that often excluded the very people who kept the city running. Addie's narrative intersects with the broader story of migration and community formation in Hartford's North End.

Interactive Spatial Narrative Component

Use the interactive map above to trace Addie's movements through Hartford's wards. Each point represents a documented residence or place of employment mentioned in her correspondence, illustrating the spatial constraints of the 19th-century domestic labor market.

Her story is not just one of struggle, but of a vibrant social network. Her relationship with the Primus family and other members of the Black elite and working class in Hartford created a safety net that allowed her to navigate a system designed to keep her stationary.

The letters between Addie and Rebecca provide an unparalleled intimate look into the emotional world of Black women during Reconstruction. They reveal how domestic space and mobility were sites of both surveillance and resistance. Through this digital mapping project, we honor Addie's footsteps as she walked the streets of Hartford, claiming space in a city that often tried to render her invisible.
